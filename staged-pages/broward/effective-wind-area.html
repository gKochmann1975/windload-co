Why Not Just Use Width x Height?

Imagine a tall, skinny window - 2 feet wide and 10 feet tall. Its actual area is only 20 square feet. But wind gusts don't just hit a tiny 2-foot strip - they sweep across the full 10-foot height simultaneously!

Real Example:

A storefront mullion is 4 inches wide and 12 feet tall. Tributary area = 4 sq ft. But effective wind area = 144 sq ft (12 x 12)!

The Span Squared Rule

ASCE 7 says to use span length squared when it's larger than the tributary area. The span is usually the longer dimension - the direction the element "spans" between supports.

For Windows:

Span = Height (floor to header)
Effective Area = MAX(W x H, H2)

The 10 Sq Ft Minimum

No matter what you calculate, ASCE 7 won't let you go below 10 square feet. A 2x2 window? Still uses 10 sq ft. A single fastener? 10 sq ft. This ensures small components are designed for localized peak gusts.

Why This Matters for Your Project

Using the larger effective wind area actually gives you LOWER pressure coefficients (remember the tributary area effect!). This can mean significant cost savings on window frames, mullions, and fasteners in Broward County's high-wind zone.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is effective wind area?

Effective wind area is the area used to determine wind pressure coefficients (GCp) from ASCE 7. It's defined as the span length squared OR the actual tributary area, whichever is LARGER. This accounts for how wind loads distribute across elements with different aspect ratios.

Why is effective wind area the span squared instead of actual area?

For long, narrow elements like mullions or purlins, the span squared better represents how wind gusts load the element. A 2-foot wide by 10-foot tall window mullion sees gusts across its full 10-foot height, even though its tributary area is only 20 sq ft. Using span squared (100 sq ft) gives more realistic pressure coefficients.

How do I calculate effective wind area for a window?

For a window, calculate both: (1) Tributary area = width x height, and (2) Span squared = span length x span length. The span is usually the larger dimension. Use whichever result is LARGER. For a 4ft x 6ft window: tributary = 24 sf, span squared = 36 sf. Use 36 sf.

What is the span length for wind load calculations?

Span length is the dimension the element spans between supports. For a window frame, it's usually the height (floor to header). For roof purlins, it's the spacing between rafters. For wall studs, it's the floor-to-ceiling height. This dimension determines how wind loads are collected.

What is the minimum effective wind area in ASCE 7?

ASCE 7 sets 10 square feet as the minimum effective wind area for looking up GCp coefficients. Even if your calculated effective wind area is smaller, you must use 10 sq ft. This ensures small components like fasteners are designed for worst-case localized pressures.
