SSG Curtain Wall Engineering

Miami-Dade Structural Silicone Glazing Wind Design

Structural silicone glazed (SSG) curtain wall systems bond glass to aluminum frames using structural silicone sealant with a design tensile strength of 20 psi, replacing mechanical captures to create flush facades. In Miami-Dade's HVHZ with 180 MPH design wind speed, SSG joint bite depth must be engineered to resist component and cladding pressures per ASCE 7-22 Section 30, with silicone meeting ASTM C1184 and system NOA approval for large missile impact zones.

Silicone Joint Failure = Glass Fallout Risk

When structural silicone bonds fail under negative wind pressure, glass panels can detach from the curtain wall frame entirely. Unlike mechanical captures that provide a secondary retention path, SSG relies solely on the adhesive bond. Undersized bite depth or incompatible substrates can lead to progressive peel failure and catastrophic glass loss during hurricanes.

SSG Bite Depth Engineering

The critical dimension that determines whether your silicone joint survives Miami-Dade hurricane loads

4-Sided SSG Bite Formula
B = (P x S) / (2 x fs)
B = bite depth, P = wind pressure, S = shorter glass span, fs = silicone design strength

In a 4-sided SSG system, wind pressure is shared by all four edges of the silicone perimeter. The formula uses the shorter glass dimension because the panel deflects in a dish shape, with the shorter span controlling the tributary area delivered to each silicone edge.

Example: 5' x 7' panel, -65 psf (ASCE 7-22 C&C)

B = (65 psf x 60 in) / (2 x 20 psi)

B = 3,900 / 40

B = 97.5 mm (3.84 in) minimum bite

2-Sided SSG Bite Formula
B = (P x S) / (2 x fs)
Same formula, but S = full span between SSG edges (longer dimension if SSG on short sides)

With only two bonded edges carrying wind load, the entire tributary width between the SSG joints determines the load delivered to each silicone line. This dramatically increases the required bite depth compared to 4-sided SSG for the same glass size.

Example: 5' x 7' panel, -65 psf (SSG on long sides)

B = (65 psf x 60 in) / (2 x 20 psi)

B = 3,900 / 40

B = 97.5 mm (3.84 in) — same panel but carries all load on 2 edges

Key difference: In 2-sided SSG, the two non-bonded edges use mechanical pressure plates. This provides a backup retention path but eliminates the flush exterior on those edges.

2-Sided vs 4-Sided SSG Systems

Choosing between partial and full structural silicone bonding for Miami-Dade HVHZ curtain walls

2-Sided SSG
Silicone on 2 edges + mechanical captures on 2 edges
  • Easier quality control during fabrication
  • Mechanical backup retention on 2 edges
  • Simpler NOA testing requirements
  • Visible pressure plate on 2 edges
  • Higher stress per silicone edge
  • Common in mid-rise applications
4-Sided SSG
Structural silicone bonds on all 4 edges
  • Completely flush exterior aesthetic
  • Load shared across all 4 edges equally
  • Better glass retention after impact
  • Reduced bite depth per edge
  • Requires factory-applied silicone (no field glazing)
  • More rigorous NOA testing for HVHZ

ASCE 7-22 Component & Cladding Loads for SSG Curtain Walls

Structural silicone glazing joints must be designed for the negative (suction) component and cladding (C&C) pressures calculated per ASCE 7-22 Chapter 30, not the lower MWFRS pressures from Chapter 27. This distinction is critical because C&C pressures govern localized cladding design and are significantly higher than MWFRS pressures, particularly at building corners and roof edges.

Why Negative Pressure Controls SSG Design

When wind flows over a building, negative pressure (suction) develops on leeward walls, side walls, and roof surfaces. This suction acts outward, trying to pull the glass away from the frame. For SSG systems, this places the structural silicone in direct tension, which is the most critical loading condition. Positive pressure pushes glass inward against the frame, where the silicone is in compression and the aluminum framing provides backup support.

Per ASCE 7-22 Section 30.4, enclosed building C&C pressures use the equation p = qh[(GCp) - (GCpi)]. For Miami-Dade HVHZ with V = 180 mph and Exposure C at 150 feet height, velocity pressure qh can exceed 75 psf. Combined with GCp coefficients of -1.4 for wall Zone 5 (corner) areas with effective wind area under 10 sq ft, the resulting negative design pressures can reach -90 to -110 psf for upper-floor corner panels.

Effective Wind Area Considerations

The effective wind area for an SSG panel equals the tributary area of the panel itself, not the entire curtain wall. Per ASCE 7-22 Section 26.2, effective wind area is the span length multiplied by the effective width (span/3 or actual panel width, whichever is smaller). For a typical 5 x 7 ft SSG panel, the effective wind area is approximately 35 sq ft, which falls in the intermediate range on the GCp charts and yields pressures between the small-area maximum and large-area minimum.

Engineers must calculate C&C pressures for each unique panel location on the building face. Corner zone panels (Zone 5) require significantly higher design pressures than interior zone panels (Zone 4). The SSG joint must be sized for the worst-case panel in each zone, with bite depth calculated separately for corner versus field conditions.

ASTM Standards Governing Structural Silicone

Three ASTM standards form the backbone of SSG material qualification and design methodology. Each addresses a different aspect of ensuring the structural silicone bond performs reliably over the building's service life.

ASTM C1184: Standard Specification for Structural Silicone Sealants

ASTM C1184 establishes the minimum physical property requirements for silicone sealants used in structural glazing applications. The standard requires minimum tensile strength of 50 psi (345 kPa) per ASTM C1135 testing, minimum elongation of 25%, and documented movement capability of at least +/- 12.5%. Critically, the standard also requires adhesion-in-peel testing per ASTM C794 to verify the silicone maintains cohesive failure mode (fails within the silicone body) rather than adhesive failure (peels away from the substrate). Cohesive failure indicates proper surface preparation and compatibility.

ASTM C1401: Guide for Structural Sealant Glazing

ASTM C1401 provides comprehensive engineering guidance for SSG system design, including the bite depth calculation methodology, safety factor requirements, and quality control procedures. The standard specifies a minimum safety factor of 2.5 applied to the ultimate tensile strength to derive the design strength. With ASTM C1184 requiring minimum 50 psi ultimate strength, the resulting design strength is 50 / 2.5 = 20 psi (138 kPa). This safety factor accounts for long-term weathering, manufacturing variability, installation tolerances, and cyclic fatigue over the building's design life.

ASTM C1087: Compatibility Testing

Before any SSG project proceeds, ASTM C1087 compatibility testing must be performed between the structural silicone and every material it contacts: glass coatings, aluminum anodizing or paint, spacer tapes, setting blocks, gaskets, and weather seals. Incompatible materials can cause the silicone to lose adhesion, discolor, soften, or stiffen over time. Miami-Dade NOA applications require documented compatibility test results as part of the approval package, and the silicone manufacturer typically performs these tests with a project-specific compatibility letter.

Silicone Joint Geometry Requirements

Beyond bite depth, ASTM C1401 specifies critical joint proportions. The silicone thickness (glueline) must be at least 6 mm (1/4 inch) and typically should not exceed the bite depth. The thickness controls the silicone's ability to accommodate differential thermal movement between glass and frame without overstressing the bond. The contact width (wetted surface on each substrate) must be at least 6 mm on both the glass and frame surfaces. Joint aspect ratios (bite to thickness) should generally stay between 1:1 and 3:1 for optimal stress distribution.

Miami-Dade NOA Requirements for SSG Systems

HVHZ approval demands system-level testing that goes beyond standard ASTM performance testing

TAS 201: Large Missile Impact
  • 9-lb 2x4 lumber at 50 fps into SSG panel
  • Impact at center and within 6 inches of silicone joint
  • Glass must crack but silicone bond retains panel
  • No through-penetration or glass fallout allowed
  • Laminated glass with SSG shows superior retention
TAS 202: Cyclic Pressure
  • 4,500 positive cycles + 4,500 negative cycles
  • Pressure at 50% of design load
  • Simulates years of wind fatigue on silicone bond
  • No silicone delamination or permanent deformation
  • Post-cycling adhesion pull test must pass
TAS 203: Structural Load
  • Uniform pressure applied to 1.5x design pressure
  • Held for 10 seconds at peak load
  • No glass breakage or silicone bond failure
  • Deflection measured and compared to span/175 limit
  • Performed after impact and cyclic sequences
System-Level Requirements
  • NOA must cover complete assembly (glass + silicone + frame)
  • Substituting any component voids the NOA
  • Factory-applied silicone required for 4-sided SSG
  • Compatibility testing per ASTM C1087 documented
  • Annual renewal with ongoing quality audits

Glass Retention Advantage: SSG vs Mechanical Capture

One of the most compelling arguments for structural silicone glazing in hurricane zones is the superior glass retention performance after impact. When a large missile strikes laminated glass in a curtain wall, the glass cracks but the interlayer holds the fragments together. What happens next depends on how the glass is attached to the frame, and this is where SSG systems demonstrate a measurable advantage.

Continuous Bond vs Point Loading

Mechanical pressure plate systems grip the glass edge at discrete fastener locations, typically spaced 6 to 9 inches apart. When cracked laminated glass deflects inward under subsequent wind pressure cycles, the glass pulls against these point loads, creating stress concentrations in the interlayer at each fastener. Over thousands of pressure cycles during a hurricane, the interlayer can tear at these stress points, leading to progressive glass fallout.

SSG systems distribute the retention force continuously along the entire perimeter of the structural silicone bond. The flexible silicone allows the cracked panel to deflect and recover without concentrated stress points. Field observations after Hurricanes Andrew (1992), Irma (2017), and Ian (2022) documented that SSG curtain walls retained cracked laminated glass at rates exceeding 95%, compared to variable retention rates with mechanical capture systems depending on fastener spacing and interlayer thickness.

Post-Impact Cyclic Performance

The Miami-Dade TAS testing sequence is specifically designed to evaluate this retention capability. After the large missile impact per TAS 201, the same specimen undergoes 9,000 pressure cycles per TAS 202 at alternating positive and negative loads. The SSG bond must hold the cracked glass through every cycle. This sequential testing protocol is more demanding than the separate ASTM E1886/E1996 impact and cycling tests used in non-HVHZ Florida zones, which is why HVHZ NOA approval carries more weight with specifying architects and building officials.

UV Degradation and Long-Term Silicone Durability

Structural silicone sealants meeting ASTM C1184 are formulated with UV stabilizers that prevent molecular chain breakdown from solar exposure. Accelerated weathering tests per ASTM C1442 demonstrate that properly formulated structural silicones retain at least 75% of original tensile strength after equivalent exposure exceeding 20 years. The 2.5x safety factor from ASTM C1401 explicitly accounts for this long-term degradation, ensuring the joint maintains adequate capacity throughout the building's service life.

However, field performance depends critically on proper surface preparation during fabrication. Contaminated substrates, incorrect primer application, or exposure to incompatible cleaning solvents can accelerate adhesive failure beyond the design envelope. This is why Miami-Dade NOA requirements emphasize factory-controlled application for 4-sided SSG and mandate documented quality control procedures including destructive peel testing of production samples at frequencies specified by the silicone manufacturer.

SSG Curtain Wall Wind Design FAQ

Common engineering questions about structural silicone glazing in Miami-Dade HVHZ

What is the typical design strength of structural silicone sealant for SSG curtain walls?
Structural silicone sealant used in SSG curtain wall systems has a typical design tensile strength of 20 psi (138 kPa). This value is derived from the ultimate tensile strength of approximately 50 psi divided by a safety factor of 2.5 per ASTM C1401. The 20 psi design strength applies to both two-component and one-component structural silicones meeting ASTM C1184 requirements. Some manufacturers offer high-performance formulations with ultimate strengths exceeding 60 psi, yielding design strengths up to 24 psi, which can reduce required bite depth by approximately 20% for the same wind load.
How do you calculate bite depth for a structural silicone glazing joint?
Bite depth for an SSG joint is calculated using the formula: Bite = (Wind Pressure x Shorter Span) / (2 x Silicone Design Strength). For a 4-sided SSG system with -70 psf wind pressure, 5-foot glass short dimension, and 20 psi silicone design strength: Bite = (70 x 60 inches) / (2 x 20) = 4,200 / 40 = 105 mm (4.13 inches) minimum. You must then add tolerances for glass size variation (typically +/- 1/16 inch per ASTM C1036), frame fabrication tolerances, and a practical rounding-up to the next standard extrusion dimension. Most Miami-Dade HVHZ high-rise projects require bite depths ranging from 25 mm (1 inch) for smaller panels in field zones to 50+ mm (2+ inches) for large corner zone panels.
What is the difference between 2-sided and 4-sided structural silicone glazing?
In 2-sided SSG, structural silicone bonds the glass to the frame on two opposite edges (typically the vertical mullions), while the other two edges use mechanical captures like toggle or screw-applied pressure plates. In 4-sided SSG, all four edges are bonded with structural silicone, creating a completely flush exterior with no visible frame or pressure plates. Four-sided SSG distributes wind load to all four perimeter joints equally, reducing the load delivered to each silicone line compared to 2-sided where only two edges carry the full suction load. The trade-off is that 4-sided SSG must be factory-glazed under controlled conditions, requires more rigorous NOA testing for HVHZ approval, and eliminates the mechanical backup retention path that pressure plates provide on the non-bonded edges of 2-sided systems.
Does Miami-Dade HVHZ require special NOA approval for SSG curtain walls?
Yes. Miami-Dade HVHZ requires a Notice of Acceptance (NOA) specifically covering the SSG system as a complete assembly, not just individual components. The NOA must demonstrate compliance with TAS 201 (large missile impact with a 9-lb 2x4 at 50 fps), TAS 202 (9,000 uniform pressure cycles at 50% of design load), and TAS 203 (uniform structural load at 1.5x design pressure). The critical aspect for SSG is that the silicone bond must survive the complete sequential test protocol: impact first, then pressure cycling, then structural overload. Any silicone delamination, glass fallout, or permanent joint deformation during any phase results in test failure. Substituting a different silicone brand, glass makeup, or frame alloy from the tested assembly voids the NOA entirely.
Can SSG systems provide better glass retention than mechanically captured glazing during hurricanes?
In many observed cases, yes. When impact-resistant laminated glass cracks from large missile impact, the structural silicone bond distributes the post-breakage retention force continuously along the entire panel perimeter. Mechanical pressure plate systems concentrate the retention force at discrete fastener points spaced 6 to 9 inches apart, creating stress concentrations in the interlayer that can initiate tearing during thousands of subsequent wind pressure cycles. The flexible silicone accommodates the inward deflection of cracked laminated glass without the point-load concentrations. Post-hurricane damage assessments after Andrew, Irma, and Ian documented SSG curtain wall glass retention rates exceeding 95% when the original silicone bond was intact and properly applied. However, this advantage depends entirely on proper surface preparation, compatible materials, and factory-controlled application quality.
How does UV exposure and weathering affect structural silicone joint strength over time?
Structural silicone sealants formulated per ASTM C1184 include UV stabilizers that resist molecular chain scission from solar radiation. Accelerated weathering tests per ASTM C1442 demonstrate that qualified products retain at least 75% of their original tensile strength after exposure equivalent to 20+ years of South Florida conditions. The 2.5x safety factor mandated by ASTM C1401 explicitly accommodates this degradation, so even at 75% retained strength the joint still exceeds design requirements. The more significant durability risk is not UV alone but the combination of UV, heat cycling, and chemical exposure from aggressive cleaning agents. Alkali-based glass cleaners, acidic facade wash solutions, and solvent-based graffiti removers can attack the silicone-to-substrate adhesion interface. Miami-Dade NOA documentation typically includes a list of approved cleaning products and prohibited chemicals that the building maintenance team must follow to preserve warranty coverage and long-term structural integrity.
