Architectural Character and Structural DNA

Two decorative styles, two fundamentally different engineering approaches to wind resistance

Colonial Shutters

Colonial shutters mount on vertical hinges flanking each window, replicating the louvered plantation-style look homeowners across Broward County's inland communities favor. When deployed, two equal panels swing inward to meet at the center, where a slide-bolt latch locks them together. The dual-hinge design splits wind load across four or more attachment points per panel, enabling stronger design pressure ratings for wider openings.

  • MountingSide-hinged (vertical)
  • Panel Count2 per window
  • Typical MDP++80 to +120 psf
  • Typical MDP--100 to -150 psf
  • Shade BenefitNone when open
  • Best Style MatchMediterranean, Colonial

Bahama Shutters

Bahama shutters are single-panel systems that mount at the window header on a horizontal hinge bar. In everyday use, the panel props open at a 30 to 45 degree outward angle, creating a permanent awning that blocks direct sun while admitting light and breeze. During a storm, the support arms release and the panel drops flat against the window, secured by thumb screws or barrel bolts at the sill. This top-hinge cantilever design carries the full wind moment vertically.

  • MountingTop-hinged (horizontal)
  • Panel Count1 per window
  • Typical MDP++60 to +100 psf
  • Typical MDP--80 to -120 psf
  • Shade BenefitSignificant (passive cooling)
  • Best Style MatchTropical, Coastal, Key West

The Passive Cooling Advantage Bahama Shutters Deliver

Why Broward County homeowners save hundreds on AC bills with a shutter style originally designed for Caribbean heat

Engineering Sun Control Into Storm Protection

Bahama shutters propped open at a 35-degree angle block 65 to 80 percent of direct solar radiation while maintaining clear outward visibility and allowing prevailing southeast breezes to flow through the louver gaps. In Broward County, where summer temperatures routinely exceed 92 degrees Fahrenheit and cooling accounts for 40 to 50 percent of residential electricity bills, this passive shading translates directly into lower utility costs.

Colonial shutters cannot provide this benefit. When open, they fold flat against the exterior wall and shade nothing. They become purely decorative until a storm forces deployment. Bahama shutters work for you 365 days a year.

Storm Preparation: Step-by-Step Deployment

Both styles deploy without professional help or ladders on single-story homes

Colonial Deployment

  • 1 Release hold-backs: Unhook the decorative hold-back hardware that keeps each panel flat against the wall during non-storm conditions.
  • 2 Swing panels inward: Pull the left and right panels toward the center of the window. Each panel pivots on its vertical hinge assembly.
  • 3 Engage center latch: Align the center meeting rail and engage the slide bolt. Insert the hurricane bar or keeper pin for positive locking.
  • 4 Secure top and bottom bolts: Drive the barrel bolts at the header and sill into their receiver cups. Check all four corners for tight engagement.
20-30 min for 12 windows

Bahama Deployment

  • 1 Disconnect support arms: Release the adjustable arm pins that hold the panel at its shade angle. The arm either swings in or telescopes closed.
  • 2 Lower the panel: Gravity does the work. Guide the panel down to rest flat against the window frame, pivoting on the top-mounted hinge bar.
  • 3 Lock at sill: Insert thumb screws or turn barrel bolts into the pre-drilled sill anchors. Two to four fasteners depending on panel width.
10-15 min for 12 windows

Why the Wind Load Path Matters for Your Selection

The structural engineering reason colonial shutters achieve higher design pressures at equivalent sizes

Colonial: Split-Span Load Distribution

When wind impacts a deployed colonial shutter, the pressure distributes across two separate panels. Each panel transfers its load through side-mounted hinges into the structural framing on one side. The center latch provides mid-span bracing, effectively halving the unsupported horizontal span. This shorter lever arm means each hinge point carries less bending moment, allowing colonial shutters to achieve higher design pressures for wider openings. For a 48-inch window, each colonial panel spans only 24 inches between supports.

Structural advantage: Wider openings, higher DP ratings, less hardware stress per connection point.

Bahama: Full-Cantilever Vertical Load

A Bahama shutter's single panel carries 100 percent of the wind load through a top-mounted hinge bar. During negative pressure (suction), the entire panel acts as a cantilever beam loaded from the bottom fasteners to the header hinge. This creates a longer unbraced span and a larger bending moment at the hinge line. The result: Bahama shutters reach their maximum allowable design pressure at smaller panel sizes compared to colonials. For openings wider than 48 inches or requiring MDP ratings above -100 psf, Bahama models may not have qualifying product approvals.

Practical limit: Best suited for standard window sizes up to 48 inches wide in Broward County wind zones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Answers specific to Broward County colonial and Bahama shutter installations

Do colonial shutters meet Broward County 170 MPH wind speed requirements?
Yes, many colonial shutter systems carry Florida Product Approvals that cover Broward County's 170 MPH basic design wind speed. Colonial shutters typically achieve design pressure ratings between +80 and +120 psf positive and -100 to -150 psf negative, depending on panel size, aluminum gauge thickness, and hinge hardware. Manufacturers such as HV Aluminium and Armor Screen produce colonial models with approvals covering Broward County wind zones. Always verify that the specific Florida Product Approval number covers your exact opening dimensions and the design pressure your building requires per ASCE 7-22 calculations.
What shade advantages do Bahama shutters have over colonial shutters?
Bahama shutters provide significant passive shading that colonial shutters cannot offer. Mounted at the window header and propped open at a 30 to 45 degree angle, Bahama shutters block 65 to 80 percent of direct solar radiation while allowing natural light and airflow through louvered gaps. In Broward County's subtropical climate, this shading effect reduces interior temperatures near windows by 8 to 12 degrees Fahrenheit during peak afternoon hours and lowers annual cooling costs by 15 to 25 percent for south and west-facing rooms. Colonial shutters provide zero shade benefit when in their open (non-deployed) position because they fold completely flat against the wall on each side of the window.
How much do colonial vs Bahama shutters cost installed in Broward County?
Colonial shutters typically cost $35 to $55 per square foot installed in Broward County, while Bahama shutters range from $30 to $50 per square foot installed. For a standard 36 by 60 inch window, expect $525 to $825 for colonials and $450 to $750 for Bahamas. Colonial shutters cost more primarily because they require twice the hinge hardware (two panels vs one), a center latch assembly, and additional hold-back brackets. Whole-home pricing for 12 standard windows runs $6,300 to $9,900 for colonials versus $5,400 to $9,000 for Bahamas. Broward County building permit fees add $150 to $300 regardless of shutter style.
Which shutter style do Broward County HOAs prefer?
Most Broward County HOAs accept both colonial and Bahama shutters because they are considered decorative and architecturally complementary, unlike accordion shutters or storm panels which many HOAs restrict. Colonial shutters are particularly popular in communities with Mediterranean, Colonial Revival, or traditional architecture including Coral Springs, Parkland, and Weston. Bahama shutters are favored in tropical, coastal, and Key West-style homes, especially in Fort Lauderdale beach areas and Deerfield Beach. HOAs typically require color matching to the home exterior, may mandate aluminum over steel in coastal zones for corrosion resistance, and often prohibit mixing shutter styles on the same facade. Always submit an architectural review application and obtain written HOA approval before purchasing.
Can I deploy colonial and Bahama shutters by myself?
Yes, both styles are designed for homeowner deployment without professional assistance, which is one of their major selling points compared to removable storm panels or plywood. Colonial shutters deploy by releasing hold-back hardware, swinging two panels inward, engaging a center latch, and securing top and bottom barrel bolts at each window. A typical 12-window home takes 20 to 30 minutes. Bahama shutters are faster because each window has a single panel that drops from its propped position and locks at the bottom with two to four fasteners. The same 12-window home can be secured in 10 to 15 minutes. Neither style requires ladders for standard first-floor windows, making them practical for elderly homeowners or anyone preparing alone before a storm.
Do Broward County historic districts have special shutter rules?
Yes. Properties in designated Broward County historic districts must satisfy local historic preservation guidelines on top of the Florida Building Code wind load requirements that apply countywide. Colonial shutters are nearly always approved in historic districts because they replicate traditional louvered shutters used on historic Florida structures. Bahama shutters may require additional review depending on the architectural period the district seeks to preserve. The Sailboat Bend Historic District in Fort Lauderdale and the Deerfield Beach Historic District both require exterior modification applications reviewed by their respective historic preservation boards. Expect the review process to add 30 to 60 days beyond the standard Broward County building permit timeline. File your historic review application concurrently with the building permit to avoid sequential delays.
What is the structural load path difference between colonial and Bahama shutters?
Colonial and Bahama shutters transfer hurricane wind forces through fundamentally different structural mechanisms. Colonial shutters distribute wind pressure through side-mounted hinges on both vertical edges of each panel, with the center meeting rail providing mid-span support. This creates two shorter horizontal spans with load shared across four or more hinge points per panel, resulting in lower bending moments at each connection. Bahama shutters transfer all wind pressure through a single top-mounted hinge bar and bottom locking fasteners, creating one vertical cantilever span from header to sill. This full-cantilever loading produces higher bending moments at the hinge line, which is why Bahama shutters have lower maximum design pressures at equivalent panel sizes compared to colonials. This structural reality is the primary engineering reason colonials handle wider openings and higher DP requirements.
