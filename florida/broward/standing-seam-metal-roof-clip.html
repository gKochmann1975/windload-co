Panel Mechanics

Snap-Lock vs Mechanical Seam Engagement

The method by which standing seam panels connect to clips determines the maximum achievable wind uplift resistance.

⚙

Snap-Lock

FM 1-60 to FM 1-90

Panels snap together by hand. Male rib locks into female receiver with a friction fit. Fastest installation but limited to field zones in moderate wind regions. Not recommended for Broward County corner zones without engineered verification.

⚗

Single-Lock Mechanical

FM 1-90 to FM 1-120

Power seamer folds the male leg over the female leg at 90 degrees. Provides positive engagement that resists clip disengagement under uplift. Suitable for perimeter zones in Broward County at appropriate clip spacing.

⚖

Double-Lock Mechanical

FM 1-120 to FM 1-150+

Power seamer folds the connection 180 degrees, creating an interlocking hook that cannot separate under uplift. Required for Broward County corner and perimeter zones on buildings over 30 feet in height. Achieves the highest wind uplift resistance of any standing seam profile.

The engagement mechanism between the clip and the panel seam is fundamentally what separates standing seam systems from through-fastened metal roofing. A standing seam clip grips the underside of the male panel rib, and the seam closure — whether snap-lock or mechanically seamed — locks the adjacent panel down over the clip. Under wind uplift, the load path transfers from the panel surface through the seam to the clip, through the clip's fasteners into the structural deck. If the seam disengages from the clip before the clip's fastener pulls out, the seam becomes the weak link and the system fails at a lower load than the clip's rated capacity.

In Broward County, where design wind speeds reach 170 MPH for Risk Category II buildings and 180 MPH for essential facilities, mechanical seaming is the standard for commercial and most residential construction. Snap-lock profiles can be used in the field zone of low-rise residential buildings where calculated uplift pressures remain below the tested snap-lock capacity, but the narrow margin makes mechanical seaming the safer engineering choice. Double-lock mechanical seams are mandatory for any roof area where the calculated design pressure exceeds -90 psf — which includes virtually all perimeter and corner zones in coastal Broward locations.

Tributary Area Calculation

Load per clip = Design Pressure (psf) x Panel Width (ft) x Clip Spacing (ft). For a 16-inch (1.33 ft) panel with clips at 24" (2.0 ft) o.c. and -60 psf uplift: Load = 60 x 1.33 x 2.0 = 160 lbs per clip. The clip's tested capacity must exceed this value with the safety factor required by the applicable code and product approval.

Thermal Movement

Fixed Clips vs Floating Clips

Standing seam panels expand and contract with temperature. The clip system must accommodate this thermal movement while maintaining full wind uplift resistance.

Fixed Clips

Anchor Points Only

Fixed clips rigidly connect the panel to the structural deck, creating an immovable anchor point. They are used sparingly — typically one per panel run at a designated bearing point — to establish a reference location from which thermal expansion occurs in both directions. Over-using fixed clips across a long panel run prevents thermal movement and causes oil-canning, fastener back-out, and clip fatigue failure.

  • Used at ridge, eave, or mid-span anchor points
  • Zero lateral movement tolerance
  • Maximum uplift resistance (full fastener engagement)
  • Limits panel expansion to one direction from anchor
  • Spacing: one per panel at designated anchor point

Floating Clips

Thermal Expansion Accommodation

Floating clips allow the panel to slide laterally over the clip base while maintaining full uplift grip on the panel rib. The clip's upper jaw grips the panel seam, while the lower base remains fastened to the deck. A channel or slot allows 1/4" to 1" of lateral movement depending on clip design. In Broward County, where roof surface temperatures routinely swing from 70 degrees F overnight to 170 degrees F in direct sun, a 40-foot aluminum panel can move up to 0.48 inches.

  • Used at all non-anchor clip locations
  • Allows 1/4" to 1" lateral movement per clip
  • Full uplift resistance maintained during thermal cycling
  • Prevents oil-canning and panel distortion
  • Requires smooth substrate for slide action

The thermal coefficient of expansion for steel is approximately 6.5 x 10^-6 in/in/degree F, while aluminum is nearly twice that at 12.8 x 10^-6 in/in/degree F. For a 30-foot steel panel run in Broward County experiencing a 100 degree F temperature differential between overnight lows and peak solar heating, the calculated thermal movement is approximately 0.23 inches. For the same length in aluminum, the movement reaches 0.46 inches. This movement is not optional — if the clip system does not accommodate it, the resulting forces will either buckle the panel (oil-canning), break the clip, or pull the fasteners out of the deck substrate.

Floating clip design quality varies significantly between manufacturers. The highest-performing floating clips use a two-piece design where the upper engagement jaw is entirely separate from the fastened base, connected only by a slide channel. Lower-cost one-piece clips with a bent tab or slot provide some movement tolerance but can bind under load, reducing both the available thermal travel and the effective uplift capacity. When specifying clips for Broward County projects, verify that the product approval testing was conducted on the specific floating clip model at the proposed spacing — uplift capacity tested with a fixed clip cannot be applied to a floating clip of the same manufacturer without separate testing data.

Testing & Certification

Clip Pull-Out Testing: UL 580 & UL 1897

Wind uplift resistance of standing seam systems is verified through standardized testing protocols that measure both individual clip capacity and full-assembly performance.

UL 580 — Full Assembly Test

Tests a complete roof assembly (panels + clips + seams + substrate) in a pressure chamber measuring 5 ft x 9 ft minimum. The assembly is subjected to increasing uplift pressure until failure occurs. The test classifies performance as UL 30 (30 psf), UL 60 (60 psf), or UL 90 (90 psf). Broward County's typical requirement of FM 1-90 or higher exceeds UL 580's standard classifications, which is why FM Global testing is the prevailing standard for high-wind metal roofing.

Test Pressure Range90 psf max standard

UL 1897 — Individual Clip Test

Tests the pull-out resistance of a single clip from its substrate attachment. The clip is fastened to the specified deck material (steel, wood, concrete) and loaded in pure tension until the clip pulls free or the fastener fails. Results are reported as ultimate load in pounds. Typical high-capacity clips tested per UL 1897 achieve 250-450 lbs ultimate pull-out in 22-gauge steel deck. This test isolates clip capacity from seam strength, allowing engineers to identify the governing failure mode.

Typical Clip Capacity250-450 lbs ultimate
Governing Failure Mode

Seam Strength vs Clip Strength

A standing seam roof system fails at whichever component reaches its limit first. Understanding the governing failure mode is essential for correct specification in Broward County.

There are three potential failure modes in a standing seam roof under wind uplift, and the system capacity is governed by whichever fails first. Clip pull-out occurs when the fastener connecting the clip to the structural deck withdraws from the substrate — this is governed by fastener type, deck material, and deck thickness. Clip deformation occurs when the clip body bends or fractures, releasing the panel seam — this is governed by clip material gauge and geometry. Seam disengagement occurs when the panel-to-panel connection separates under uplift load, allowing the panel to lift off the clip — this is governed by seam type (snap-lock vs mechanical) and seam integrity.

In most properly designed systems for Broward County, the governing failure mode is clip pull-out from the deck substrate. A well-formed double-lock mechanical seam has higher capacity than the clip beneath it, and the clip fastener's pull-out resistance from the deck establishes the system's ceiling. However, if a snap-lock seam is used, seam disengagement typically governs — the seam separates at 60-90 psf while the clip could have held 150+ psf. This mismatch is why snap-lock profiles are restricted to field zones with moderate uplift pressures.

Clip Height & Insulation Thickness

Clip height must accommodate the insulation thickness between the structural deck and the metal panels. Standard clips are 1-1/2" to 2" tall for uninsulated applications. When rigid insulation board is placed over the deck, clips must extend through the insulation to engage the structural substrate. Available heights include 2", 3", 4", and 6" — with taller clips requiring stiffer material gauges to prevent clip body deflection under uplift load. The taller the clip, the greater the bending moment at the base, which reduces effective pull-out capacity unless the clip is designed with a wider base plate or additional fasteners.

Load Distribution

How Panel Width Affects Clip Tributary Area

Wider panels assign more wind load to each clip. Understanding tributary area is fundamental to clip spacing design.

The tributary area of a standing seam clip is the rectangle defined by the panel width times the clip spacing. Each clip is responsible for resisting the wind uplift acting on this area. The calculation is straightforward: Clip Load (lbs) = Design Pressure (psf) x Panel Width (ft) x Clip Spacing (ft). What makes this deceptively simple equation critical in Broward County is that small changes in panel width produce proportionally large changes in clip load — and those changes compound in the high-pressure corner and perimeter zones.

Consider two common panel widths at the same 18-inch clip spacing in a corner zone with -120 psf design pressure. A 12-inch (1.0 ft) panel puts 180 lbs on each clip, while a 16-inch (1.33 ft) panel puts 240 lbs on the same clip. That 33% increase in panel width translates directly to a 33% increase in clip load. If the clip is rated at 250 lbs, the narrower panel leaves 28% margin while the wider panel leaves only 4% — an engineering red flag that demands either closer spacing or a higher-capacity clip. Many standing seam failures in hurricane events trace back to this basic miscalculation: the clip spacing was designed for one panel width, but a wider panel was installed during construction.

Panel widths available for standing seam systems in the Florida market range from 12 inches to 24 inches, with 16-inch and 18-inch profiles being the most common for commercial applications. Narrower panels (12-inch) offer advantages in high-wind zones because each clip carries less load, but they increase material cost per square foot due to more seams and more clips. Wider panels (18-24 inch) reduce labor but demand closer clip spacing and stronger clips, often eliminating any cost savings. For Broward County, 16-inch panels with double-lock mechanical seams represent the dominant specification for commercial standing seam roofing.

Re-Roofing

Installing Standing Seam Over Existing Substrate

Re-roofing with standing seam metal over an existing roof requires careful evaluation of the substrate's capacity to anchor clips under Broward County's wind loads.

1

Structural Capacity Verification

A licensed engineer must verify the existing roof structure can support the new metal roof system plus wind loads. Dead load addition is typically 1.0-1.5 psf for standing seam, but the critical check is whether the existing deck can develop adequate clip pull-out resistance.

2

Substrate Penetration Depth

Clip fasteners must penetrate through existing roofing material, through the sheathing, and into structural framing members. Fasteners anchored only in plywood sheathing achieve 50-80 lbs pull-out — far below the 250+ lbs needed for Broward wind zones. Framing member engagement is non-negotiable.

3

Maximum Roof Coverings

FBC Section 1511.3 limits buildings to a maximum of two roof coverings. If the existing roof already has two layers, full tear-off is required before the standing seam system can be installed. A Broward County inspector will verify layer count during the permit process.

4

Product Approval Match

The standing seam system's Florida Product Approval must cover the specific re-roof application. Many product approvals only cover direct-to-deck installation. Verify the FL number's limitations of use section explicitly permits installation over existing roofing substrates.

5

Underlayment Requirements

FBC requires a self-adhering modified bitumen underlayment or mechanically attached synthetic underlayment beneath metal roofing per Section 1507.4. In re-roof applications, this underlayment goes over the existing roof surface (after any necessary repairs) and beneath the new standing seam panels.

6

Clip Alignment to Framing

In re-roof conditions, locating structural framing beneath existing sheathing and roofing can be challenging. Electronic stud finders may not work through multiple layers. Some installers use pilot holes to verify framing location before committing clip fasteners — a step that adds time but prevents clip pull-out failures.

Broward County Requirements

Product Approval for Metal Roofing Systems

Broward County enforces the Florida Building Code with strict product approval requirements for all roofing systems installed within its jurisdiction.

Every standing seam metal roof system installed in Broward County must have a current Florida Product Approval (FL number) listed on the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) product approval database. The approval must cover the complete system as tested: panel profile, panel gauge, clip model, clip spacing, seam type, fastener type, and deck substrate. Installing any component that deviates from the tested configuration — including using a different clip model or wider clip spacing than tested — voids the product approval and will fail Broward County inspection.

The product approval process requires testing to Florida TAS standards: TAS 101 for windborne debris impact resistance (required in the Wind-Borne Debris Region, which includes all of Broward County), TAS 110 for uniform static air pressure difference, and TAS 125 for dynamic wind uplift from cyclic loading. TAS 125 is particularly relevant to standing seam systems because the cyclic loading simulates real hurricane conditions where pressure fluctuates rapidly — a scenario that can cause fatigue failure in clips that pass static testing but fail under repeated load cycling.

HVHZ Considerations

The eastern portion of Broward County falls within the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ), which triggers additional requirements beyond the standard Florida Building Code. HVHZ areas require Miami-Dade County Notice of Acceptance (NOA) approval in addition to the standard FL product approval. This dual-approval requirement catches many contractors off guard — a product with an FL number but no NOA cannot be installed in HVHZ areas of Broward County. When bidding projects near the coast, verify the project address against Broward County's HVHZ boundary maps before specifying the standing seam system.

For projects outside the HVHZ but still within Broward County, the standard FL product approval is sufficient. However, the product approval must show tested performance at or above the calculated design pressure for the specific building. Broward County building inspectors will compare the calculated wind loads from the signed and sealed engineering documents against the product approval's tested capacity for each roof zone — field, perimeter, and corner — using the exact clip spacing specified on the approved shop drawings.

Common Questions

Standing Seam Clip FAQ

What is the difference between snap-lock and mechanical seam standing seam panels?+
Snap-lock panels click together by hand during installation and typically achieve 60-90 psf uplift resistance. Mechanical seam panels require a powered seaming machine to fold the male and female legs together in either a single-lock (90-degree fold) or double-lock (180-degree fold) configuration. Double-lock mechanical seams achieve 120+ psf uplift resistance and are required in Broward County's higher wind zones, particularly coastal areas with 170+ MPH design wind speeds. The mechanical interlock creates a positive connection that cannot separate under wind uplift without physically deforming the metal.
How does clip spacing change between field, perimeter, and corner roof zones?+
Clip spacing decreases as wind pressure increases from field to perimeter to corner zones. In Broward County, typical field zone spacing is 24-36 inches on center, perimeter zones require 12-18 inches on center, and corner zones demand 6-12 inches on center. Corner zones (Zone 3 per ASCE 7-22) experience 2-3 times the uplift pressure of the field zone, requiring clips at half or one-third the spacing. The exact spacing depends on panel width, clip capacity, and the calculated design pressure for each zone. Clip spacing must never exceed the maximum tested spacing documented in the product's Florida Product Approval.
What is the difference between fixed clips and floating clips on a standing seam roof?+
Fixed clips rigidly attach the panel to the structural deck, preventing any lateral movement. Floating clips allow the panel to expand and contract with temperature changes. Metal panels can move approximately 1/8 inch per 10 feet for every 50 degrees Fahrenheit of temperature swing. In Broward County where roof surface temperatures can reach 170 degrees F, a 40-foot panel run can expand nearly 1/2 inch. Floating clips accommodate this thermal movement while maintaining full wind uplift resistance. Most standing seam systems use fixed clips only at designated anchor points and floating clips everywhere else to prevent oil-canning and clip fatigue.
How are standing seam clips tested for wind uplift resistance?+
Standing seam clips are tested under two primary standards. UL 580 tests a full roof assembly in a pressure chamber, measuring the system's collective resistance to uniform uplift pressure. UL 1897 tests individual clip pull-out strength from the deck substrate in pure tension. FM Global also conducts proprietary uplift testing and assigns ratings (1-60, 1-90, 1-120, 1-150) indicating the system can resist that pressure in pounds per square foot. For Broward County, most roof zones require FM 1-90 minimum, with corners and perimeter zones requiring FM 1-120 or FM 1-150. Florida TAS 125 adds cyclic loading to simulate real hurricane pressure fluctuations.
Does panel width affect wind load per clip in a standing seam system?+
Yes. Panel width directly determines the tributary area each clip must resist. A clip on a 16-inch wide panel at 24-inch spacing has a tributary area of 2.67 square feet, while the same clip on a 12-inch panel at 24-inch spacing has only 2.0 square feet. At a design pressure of -60 psf, the wider panel clip must resist 160 lbs versus 120 lbs for the narrower panel. Wider panels (16-18 inches) therefore require either closer clip spacing or higher-capacity clips. Many standing seam wind failures trace back to tributary area miscalculations where the clip spacing was designed for one panel width but a different width was installed.
What FM Global wind uplift rating is required in Broward County?+
Broward County's design wind speeds of 170-180 MPH typically require FM 1-90 minimum in field zones, FM 1-120 in perimeter zones, and FM 1-150 in corner zones for low-slope applications. High-slope standing seam systems above 7:12 pitch may qualify for reduced requirements due to different pressure coefficients. The exact FM rating must match or exceed the calculated design pressure for each specific roof zone based on ASCE 7-22 Chapter 30 component and cladding coefficients. Essential facilities (hospitals, fire stations, emergency shelters) classified as Risk Category IV may require FM 1-150 across all roof zones.
Can I install a standing seam metal roof over an existing substrate in Broward County?+
Yes, but the re-roofing approach must comply with FBC Section 1511 and Broward County's product approval requirements. The existing substrate must be structurally adequate to support the new system plus wind loads. Clip fasteners must penetrate through the existing material into structural framing members, not just plywood sheathing. Fasteners in plywood alone achieve only 50-80 lbs pull-out versus the 250+ lbs needed for Broward wind zones. The product approval must explicitly cover re-roof applications, and a maximum of two roof coverings is permitted per FBC 1511.3. A licensed engineer must verify the existing structure can transfer the clip forces to the structural frame.
What product approvals does Broward County require for standing seam metal roofing?+
Broward County requires Florida Building Code product approval through the Florida Product Approval system. Standing seam roof systems must have a current FL number showing compliance with TAS 101 (windborne debris impact), TAS 110 (uniform static air pressure), and TAS 125 (dynamic wind uplift). The product approval must cover the specific panel profile, clip type, clip spacing, seam type, and substrate attachment method. Systems installed in the HVHZ portions of Broward County additionally require Miami-Dade NOA approval. All product approvals must be current and listed on the DBPR product approval database. Installing components not covered by the approval voids the certification.
