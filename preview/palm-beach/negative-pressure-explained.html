How Negative Pressure Develops

The physics behind wind suction and building envelope failure

1
Wind Strikes Building
Wind flowing at 165 MPH creates positive pressure on the windward wall. This pressure pushes inward with approximately 42 psf of force, trying to compress the building envelope.
2
Flow Separation
As wind wraps around the building, it separates from surfaces. This creates low-pressure vortices on the roof, leeward walls, and especially at corners where turbulence is most intense.
3
Suction Effect
The low external pressure combined with internal pressure creates a net outward force. This suction can exceed -105 psf at roof corners, literally trying to peel building components outward.

The Science of Wind Suction

Negative pressure, commonly called suction, occurs when the air pressure on the outside of a surface is lower than the pressure inside. According to Bernoulli's principle, as wind accelerates around building corners and over roof edges, the pressure drops significantly.

This pressure differential creates an outward force that tries to pull building components away from the structure. In Palm Beach County with 165 MPH design wind speeds, these suction forces can exceed 100 psf in corner zones - equivalent to over 14,000 pounds of force trying to lift a single roof panel.

p = q x (GCp - GCpi)
Where q = velocity pressure, GCp = external pressure coefficient (negative for suction), GCpi = internal pressure coefficient

Why Roof Corners Are Most Vulnerable

ASCE 7-22 designates roof corners as Zone 3, with the highest negative pressure coefficients. For a low-slope roof (slope less than 7 degrees), the GCp at corners reaches -2.80 for small tributary areas. This is nearly three times the suction of the interior roof zone.

The intense suction at corners results from flow separation and vortex formation. As wind flows over the roof edge, it creates spinning vortices that dramatically reduce local pressure. These corner vortices are responsible for the majority of roof failures observed in post-hurricane damage assessments.

Internal Pressure: The Hidden Multiplier

When a window breaks or door fails during a hurricane, wind rushes into the building and pressurizes the interior. This internal pressure (typically +0.18 to +0.55 per ASCE 7-22) adds to the external suction, dramatically increasing the net outward force.

For an enclosed building with GCpi of +0.18, the net pressure on a roof corner can reach:

  • External: GCp = -2.80 x 42 psf = -118 psf
  • Internal: GCpi = +0.18 x 42 psf = +8 psf (pushing outward)
  • Net: -118 - 8 = -126 psf (outward force)

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about negative pressure and suction forces

Negative pressure is typically more dangerous because suction coefficients are higher than positive pressure coefficients. While windward pressure rarely exceeds +0.80 x qh, suction at roof corners can reach -2.80 x qh - over 3.5 times higher. Additionally, many building components are designed primarily to resist gravity loads (downward), making them inherently weaker against uplift forces. This is why roof failures are more common than wall failures during hurricanes.
Building height increases the velocity pressure (qh), which directly increases all pressure values including suction. The velocity pressure coefficient Kz increases with height per ASCE 7-22 Table 26.10-1. For example, at 30 feet in Exposure C, Kz = 0.98, but at 100 feet, Kz = 1.27 - a 30% increase. This means taller buildings in Palm Beach County experience proportionally higher suction forces on roof and wall surfaces.
Internal pressure increases when the building envelope is breached on the windward side. Common causes include: broken windows or doors, failed garage doors (the largest opening in most homes), improperly sealed penetrations, or intentional openings like vents. ASCE 7-22 classifies buildings as "enclosed" (GCpi = +/-0.18), "partially enclosed" (GCpi = +0.55/-0.55), or "partially open." Partially enclosed buildings have dramatically higher net suction forces due to the additive internal pressure.
Windows and doors in Zone 5 (wall corners) and Zone 3 (roof corners) require higher design pressures than interior locations. For a Palm Beach County project at 165 MPH, a Zone 5 corner window with 10 sf tributary area needs a design pressure of approximately -59 psf, while an identical interior zone window only requires -46 psf. This 28% difference means corner windows often need higher performance ratings, affecting product selection and cost.
