Glass Failure Patterns

Different stress conditions create characteristic breakage patterns that reveal the failure mechanism

Radial Pattern

Cracks radiate outward from a central point of impact or maximum stress concentration.

Cause: Point impact from debris, localized stress concentration, or center deflection failure

Edge Failure

Cracks originate from edges where microscopic flaws from cutting concentrate stress.

Cause: Edge defects, improper glazing, thermal stress at frame, or high edge stress from bending

Corner Stress

Diagonal cracks emanate from corners where two edges meet, creating stress risers.

Cause: Improper corner support, thermal expansion restraint, or combined edge stresses

The Physics of Glass Under Wind Load

Glass is a brittle material that exhibits nearly perfect elastic behavior until sudden failure. Unlike metals that deform plastically before breaking, glass stores strain energy until its tensile strength is exceeded, then releases that energy catastrophically as crack propagation. Understanding this behavior is crucial for designing glazing systems that can withstand Miami-Dade's extreme wind pressures.

When wind pressure acts on a window, the glass deflects inward (or outward for suction), creating bending stresses. The convex surface experiences tension while the concave surface experiences compression. Since glass is approximately 10 times stronger in compression than tension, failure initiates on the tension face where microscopic surface flaws act as stress concentrators.

Stress Distribution in Rectangular Panes

For a uniformly loaded rectangular glass pane with all four edges simply supported, stress distribution follows predictable patterns based on aspect ratio and boundary conditions:

  • Maximum stress location: For typical aspect ratios, maximum tensile stress occurs near the center of the long edges, not at the geometric center of the pane
  • Corner stress: Corners experience complex biaxial stress states but typically lower magnitude than edge centers for supported conditions
  • Center deflection: Maximum deflection occurs at the pane center, but this doesn't correspond to maximum stress for most boundary conditions
  • Edge quality importance: Since maximum stress often occurs at edges, edge defects from cutting significantly reduce effective strength

Engineering Note

Glass design typically uses a factor of safety of 2.5 or higher, meaning the design stress is limited to 40% or less of the glass's nominal strength. This accounts for edge quality variation, load duration effects, and the statistical nature of glass strength.

Why Laminated Glass is Essential for Hurricanes

Miami-Dade's HVHZ requires impact-rated glazing not just for direct strength, but for post-breakage performance. During a hurricane, three critical scenarios demonstrate why laminated glass is mandatory:

  • Debris impact: Windborne debris (the "large missile" test uses a 9-lb 2x4 lumber at 50 fps) will break any reasonable glass thickness. Laminated glass allows breakage but prevents breach.
  • Cyclic pressure: After impact, the broken but intact laminate must withstand thousands of pressure cycles as the storm passes. TAS 203 requires 9,000 cycles without failure.
  • Building envelope integrity: A single window breach during a hurricane can pressurize the building interior, potentially lifting the roof. Laminated glass maintains the envelope even when cracked.

The Interlayer: PVB vs. SGP

The plastic interlayer in laminated glass determines its post-breakage performance. Two primary interlayer types dominate the market:

  • PVB (Polyvinyl Butyral): The standard interlayer for decades. Good adhesion, acoustic properties, and UV resistance. Adequate for most residential applications but becomes soft at elevated temperatures.
  • SGP (SentryGlas Plus): A stiffer, stronger ionoplast interlayer. Five times the tear strength of PVB and 100 times stiffer. Maintains strength at higher temperatures. Preferred for critical applications and large spans.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does glass break in specific patterns under wind load?

Glass breaks when tensile stress exceeds its strength. Under uniform wind pressure, maximum stress occurs at the center of unsupported edges for rectangular panes, causing characteristic radial crack patterns. Corner cracks indicate edge stress concentrations, while center breaks suggest excessive deflection or pressure. The pattern reveals the failure mechanism and stress state at the moment of breakage.

What is the difference between annealed, tempered, and laminated glass for hurricane protection?

Annealed glass is standard float glass with low strength (about 6,000 psi) that breaks into large, dangerous shards. Tempered glass is heat-treated to about 24,000 psi and shatters into small cubes. Laminated glass bonds two or more lites with PVB or SGP interlayer, maintaining integrity after breakage - this is the only type suitable for hurricane protection as it prevents envelope breach.

How do impact-rated windows resist hurricane debris?

Impact-rated assemblies use laminated glass with PVB or SGP interlayers that hold fragments together after breakage. Miami-Dade testing (TAS 201, 202, 203) requires passing large missile impact (9-lb 2x4 at 50 fps) followed by cyclic pressure testing. The laminate prevents breach even when both glass lites crack, maintaining the building envelope throughout the storm.

What causes glass to fail at the edges rather than the center?

Glass edges contain microscopic flaws from cutting that concentrate stress. While maximum bending stress in uniformly loaded plates occurs near edges, these edge defects reduce local strength by 50% or more compared to the pristine interior surface. Proper edge treatment (seaming, polishing) improves edge strength but never completely eliminates this vulnerability.

What glass thickness is required for Miami-Dade's 180 MPH wind zone?

Glass thickness depends on size, aspect ratio, zone location (corner vs interior), and support conditions. A typical 4' x 5' window in Zone 3 (corner) might require minimum 9/16" laminated glass to resist -52 psf pressure, while the same size in Zone 1 might use 7/16" laminate. All impact glazing must have NOA (Notice of Acceptance) approval from Miami-Dade County.
