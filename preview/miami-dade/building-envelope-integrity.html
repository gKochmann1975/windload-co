ASCE 7-22 Enclosure Classification

How your building is classified determines internal pressure design requirements

Enclosed Building

GCpi = +/-0.18

All openings meet ASTM E1886/E1996 or protected by shutters. Building maintains integrity under design conditions.

Criteria: Total openings ≤ 4 sq ft or 1% of wall area, AND windward openings do not exceed leeward + roof by more than 10%

Partially Enclosed

GCpi = +0.55/-0.55

Windward openings exceed leeward. Building will pressurize during storm. Requires significantly stronger roof connections.

Criteria: Windward openings exceed leeward + roof by > 10% AND windward openings exceed 4 sq ft or 1% of windward wall

Open Building

GCpi = 0

Canopies, carports, covered walkways. No internal pressure differential - wind flows through freely.

Criteria: Each wall is at least 80% open. No enclosed space to pressurize.

The Physics of Envelope Failure

Building envelope integrity is perhaps the most critical factor determining whether a structure survives a hurricane. The envelope, comprising walls, roof, windows, doors, and all penetrations, must maintain a continuous barrier against wind intrusion. When this barrier fails, the consequences are immediate and often catastrophic.

ASCE 7-22 accounts for envelope performance through the internal pressure coefficient (GCpi). For enclosed buildings with impact-rated or protected openings, GCpi is +/-0.18, representing the small pressure differential between inside and outside that can occur through HVAC systems, minor leakage, and normal building movement. When the envelope breaches, particularly on the windward side, GCpi jumps to +0.55, representing full pressurization of the interior space.

The Math of Catastrophe

Consider a typical residence in Miami-Dade's 180 MPH wind zone. At design conditions:

  • Enclosed building roof uplift: External suction (GCp = -1.0) minus internal pressure (GCpi = -0.18) yields net coefficient of -0.82. At 83 psf velocity pressure, this equals approximately -68 psf uplift.
  • Breached building roof uplift: Same external suction plus internal pressure (GCpi = +0.55) yields net coefficient of -1.55. At 83 psf velocity pressure, this equals approximately -129 psf uplift.
  • The difference: Roof uplift nearly doubles from -68 psf to -129 psf, likely exceeding the roof-to-wall connection capacity that was designed for the enclosed condition.

Hurricane Andrew Lesson

Post-Hurricane Andrew analysis (1992) revealed that garage door failure was the primary initiating event for most roof losses. This led directly to Miami-Dade's current requirements for impact-rated doors or approved bracing systems on all garage doors in the HVHZ.

Why Garage Doors Fail First

Garage doors represent the Achilles heel of residential construction in hurricane zones. A typical two-car garage door presents a 16-foot wide, 7-foot tall unsupported surface, the largest opening on most homes. Several factors contribute to their vulnerability:

  • Size and span: The wide unsupported span means even moderate wind pressures create significant total force. At 25 psf, a 16' x 7' door experiences 2,800 pounds of load.
  • Track deflection: Standard garage door tracks allow significant deflection under pressure, leading to panel separation and ultimate failure.
  • Panel construction: Non-rated doors use thin gauge steel or aluminum with minimal stiffening, unable to resist design pressures.
  • Connection failure: Even if panels hold, track-to-frame connections often fail, allowing the entire assembly to blow inward.

Impact Protection Requirements

Miami-Dade's High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) addresses envelope integrity through stringent impact testing requirements. All glazed openings and entry doors must either be impact-rated or protected by approved shutters. The testing protocol includes:

  • TAS 201 (Large Missile Impact): A 9-pound, 8-foot long 2x4 lumber fired at 50 feet per second must not penetrate the assembly.
  • TAS 202 (Small Missile Impact): Ten 2-gram steel balls fired at 130 feet per second must not penetrate.
  • TAS 203 (Cyclic Pressure): After impact, the assembly must withstand 9,000 pressure cycles at design loads without failure.

These requirements ensure that even if the outer glass lite breaks, the laminated inner lite and interlayer maintain the envelope barrier throughout the storm's duration.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens when a building envelope is breached during a hurricane?

When the envelope is breached (window breaks, door fails, garage door collapses), wind enters and pressurizes the interior. This internal pressure combines with external roof suction, potentially doubling or tripling uplift forces. The result can be catastrophic roof failure, structural collapse, and total building loss within seconds to minutes of the breach.

How much does internal pressure increase roof uplift forces?

For an enclosed building, internal pressure coefficient (GCpi) is +/-0.18. When breached on the windward side, GCpi jumps to +0.55, adding approximately 45 psf of uplift in Miami-Dade's 180 MPH zone. Combined with external suction of -45 to -80 psf, total uplift can exceed 125 psf, often beyond the design capacity of roof connections designed for enclosed conditions.

Why are garage doors the most common point of envelope failure?

Garage doors present the largest unsupported opening in most homes, typically 16+ feet wide. Without proper wind-rated doors or bracing, they can buckle under wind pressure as low as 25-30 psf. Hurricane Andrew revealed that garage door failure was the primary initiator of envelope breach and subsequent roof loss, leading to current HVHZ requirements for impact-rated or braced garage doors.

What is the difference between enclosed, partially enclosed, and open building classifications?

Enclosed buildings have total openings less than 4 sq ft or 1% of wall area (GCpi = +/-0.18). Partially enclosed buildings have windward openings exceeding leeward by more than 10% (GCpi = +0.55/-0.55). Open buildings have over 80% of walls open (GCpi = 0). Classification dramatically affects internal pressure calculations and required structural capacity.

How does ASCE 7-22 account for envelope breach in design?

ASCE 7-22 requires designers to classify enclosure type based on opening distribution. In hurricane-prone regions, glazing must be impact-resistant OR the building must be designed as partially enclosed with higher internal pressures. Miami-Dade HVHZ requires impact protection, allowing enclosed classification with lower internal pressures, which is more economical and practical.
